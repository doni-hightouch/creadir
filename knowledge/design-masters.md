# Layout Design, Distilled from the Masters

A crash guide for a complete novice. Twenty ideas, no fluff. Each one: what it is, what it does to the eye, and a phrase you can drop into a design prompt.

---

## Part 1: The Big Four (the CRAP framework)

*CRAP* = Contrast, Repetition, Alignment, Proximity. Four rules governing every good layout.

**1. Contrast**
Gloss: making different things look different.
The eye goes to the biggest difference first. Big vs small, dark vs light, bold vs thin. Weak contrast makes everything shout equally, so nothing is heard.
Prompt phrase: *"strong size and weight contrast between elements"*

**2. Repetition**
Gloss: reusing the same visual choices.
Repeated colors, fonts, and spacing tell the brain "this is one thing." Repetition creates unity; randomness creates noise.
Prompt phrase: *"consistent repeated styles, colors, and spacing throughout"*

**3. Alignment**
Gloss: lining edges up on purpose.
Every element should share an edge or line with another. Invisible lines connect things; the eye feels the order without seeing it.
Prompt phrase: *"crisp alignment, all elements on shared edges"*

**4. Proximity**
Gloss: grouping related things close together.
Things placed near each other read as one group. Distance means "unrelated." Spacing IS meaning.
Prompt phrase: *"related items grouped tightly, clear gaps between groups"*

## Part 2: How the Eye Actually Works (Gestalt laws)

*Gestalt* = how brains group visual things.

**5. Figure/Ground**
Gloss: what's subject, what's background.
The brain instantly splits any image into "the thing" and "behind the thing." Confuse this split and the layout feels muddy.
Prompt phrase: *"clear figure-ground separation, subject pops from background"*

**6. Similarity**
Gloss: matching things read as related.
Same shape, same color, same size = same family. Use it to say "these buttons do similar jobs."
Prompt phrase: *"similar elements styled identically"*

**7. Closure**
Gloss: brain completes shapes left unfinished.
You can hint at a shape and the viewer's mind finishes it. This is why simple logos feel clever. Saul Bass built a career on it.
Prompt phrase: *"suggest the shape, let the eye complete it"*

**8. Common region / enclosure**
Gloss: a box makes a group.
Anything inside a border, card, or shaded area reads as one unit — even stronger than proximity.
Prompt phrase: *"use cards or containers to enclose related content"*

## Part 3: Structure (the Swiss school)

**9. The Grid** — Müller-Brockmann
Gloss: invisible columns and rows underneath.
A grid gives every element a home. The eye senses the hidden order and relaxes. "The grid is an aid, not a guarantee" — it takes practice, not just rules.
Prompt phrase: *"strict 12-column grid, everything snaps to it"*

**10. Broken-grid tension**
Gloss: deliberately breaking your own grid.
One element crossing the grid creates energy and draws the eye hard. It only works if the grid is obvious everywhere else. Order first, then one rebellion.
Prompt phrase: *"disciplined grid with one deliberate grid-breaking element"*

**11. White space (negative space)**
Gloss: the empty area around things.
Empty space is not wasted space. It gives the eye rest and makes the remaining elements feel important. Cramped = cheap. Airy = confident.
Prompt phrase: *"generous white space, let elements breathe"*

**12. Visual hierarchy**
Gloss: an order of importance, visible.
The viewer should know what to read first, second, third — without thinking. Size, position, and contrast build the ranking. No hierarchy = a wall of equal noise.
Prompt phrase: *"obvious hierarchy: one hero, clear second and third levels"*

**13. Typographic scale**
Gloss: a fixed ladder of sizes.
Don't pick font sizes randomly. Use a stepped scale (e.g. 12, 16, 24, 36, 54). Consistent jumps create rhythm the eye trusts.
Prompt phrase: *"typographic scale with clear, consistent size steps"*

**14. Vertical rhythm**
Gloss: consistent spacing between text lines.
Line spacing and block spacing repeat like a heartbeat. When rhythm breaks, the page feels subtly "off" even if nobody can say why.
Prompt phrase: *"even vertical rhythm, consistent line and block spacing"*

**15. Semantics before syntax** — Vignelli
Gloss: meaning first, styling second.
Vignelli's rule: first understand what the thing must *say* (semantics), then build the structure (syntactics), then check people actually get it (pragmatics). Decoration without meaning is noise.
Prompt phrase: *"every element earns its place by meaning"*

## Part 4: Restraint (Rand, Bass, Rams, Glaser)

**16. Reduction** — Saul Bass
Gloss: cut until only essence remains.
Strip an idea down to one simple symbol. The fewer parts, the faster the brain grabs it, and the longer it remembers it.
Prompt phrase: *"reduce to a single strong symbol"*

**17. Simplicity with wit** — Paul Rand
Gloss: simple, but with a twist.
Plain simplicity is boring. Rand's logos were simple *plus* one clever idea (the IBM eye-bee-M). The twist is what makes it stick.
Prompt phrase: *"minimal design with one witty visual idea"*

**18. As little design as possible** — Dieter Rams
Gloss: remove everything not essential.
Rams's tenth principle. Every added element must justify itself. Unobtrusive design serves the content; loud design serves the designer's ego.
Prompt phrase: *"nothing decorative, every element functional"*

**19. Just enough** — Milton Glaser
Gloss: not minimal, not maximal — right.
Glaser's pushback on "less is more": *"Just enough is more."* Sometimes warmth, color, or ornament is exactly what the message needs. Restraint is a tool, not a religion.
Prompt phrase: *"just enough — warm but never cluttered"*

**20. One focal point**
Gloss: a single place eyes land.
Every layout needs one boss. If two elements compete to be first, both lose. Decide the hero before you place anything else.
Prompt phrase: *"one dominant focal point, everything else supports it"*

## How a Designer Critiques a Layout

The silent questions, in the order they're asked:

1. **Where does my eye go first?** And is that the right place?
2. **What is this trying to say?** Can I get it in three seconds?
3. **What's the hierarchy?** Can I rank first, second, third without effort?
4. **What lines up with what?** Are the alignments deliberate or accidental?
5. **Are the groups honest?** Does spacing match how the content is actually related?
6. **What can I remove?** Would the message survive without this element? Then cut it.
7. **Is anything competing?** Two heroes, two focal points, two loud colors fighting?
8. **Does it feel like one thing?** Or a pile of parts that happen to share a page?

Critique vocabulary you'll hear: *"it's fighting for attention," "the hierarchy is flat," "this feels orphaned," "the spacing is doing nothing," "what's anchoring this?", "it doesn't hang together."* Notice: designers critique what the layout *does to the eye*, never just whether it's pretty.

## 10 Sayings of the Masters

1. **"Design is the silent ambassador of your brand."** — Paul Rand
   Plain: your layout speaks before your words do.

2. **"Don't try to be original, just try to be good."** — Paul Rand
   Plain: clarity beats novelty every time.

3. **"Good design is obvious. Great design is transparent."** — Paul Rand (after Joe Sparano)
   Plain: the best design is invisible; you only notice the message.

4. **"The grid system is an aid, not a guarantee."** — Josef Müller-Brockmann
   Plain: structure helps, but judgment still decides.

5. **"The life of a designer is a life of fight against the ugliness."** — Massimo Vignelli
   Plain: care about visual quality everywhere, always.

6. **"If you can design one thing, you can design everything."** — Massimo Vignelli
   Plain: the principles are universal; only the medium changes.

7. **"Design is thinking made visual."** — Saul Bass
   Plain: a layout is an argument, not decoration.

8. **"Good design is as little design as possible."** — Dieter Rams
   Plain: when in doubt, take something away.

9. **"Just enough is more."** — Milton Glaser
   Plain: don't strip out the soul chasing minimalism.

10. **"God is in the details."** — Mies van der Rohe (Vignelli's mentor's creed)
    Plain: tiny misalignments and sloppy spacing betray the whole.

---

## The One-Sentence Summary

Decide the message, pick one hero, build a grid, group by meaning, size by importance, repeat your choices, and delete everything else.
