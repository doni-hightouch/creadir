# Headline & Ad Copywriting: The Craft of the Line

A headline is the sentence on the ad that does the selling. David Ogilvy's research: five times as many people read the headline as the body copy. If the headline doesn't work, nothing under it matters.

---

## 1. How to judge a headline

Six plain tests. Run every line through all six.

1. **The stranger test.** Would a busy stranger stop for this? Not your client, not your team — someone flipping past it. If it needs the logo to make sense, it fails.
2. **The specificity test.** Does it contain a fact, number, or detail you could check? "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise comes from the electric clock" beats "The world's finest motor car." Specifics are believed; superlatives are ignored.
3. **The picture test.** Can you see something when you read it? A line that puts an image in the head sticks. "Melts in your mouth, not in your hands" — you can see it.
4. **The swap test.** Could a competitor run the same line over their logo? If yes, the line says nothing true about *this* brand. It's wallpaper.
5. **The out-loud test.** Say it to a friend across a table. If you'd feel silly saying it ("Unleash synergistic wellness"), it's not human speech and won't be read as such.
6. **The so-what test.** After reading it, does the reader gain anything — a benefit, a piece of news, a promise, a laugh with a point? Cleverness with no payoff is a shrug.

## 2. Twelve line-craft rules

1. **Lead with the benefit, not the product.** *Before:* "Our drill has a titanium bit." *After:* "A hole in ten seconds."
2. **Use one-syllable words where you can — they're how people talk.** *Before:* "Facilitate your relocation." *After:* "We move you."
3. **Use active verbs; kill "is," "are," and passives.** *Before:* "Savings are provided by our plan." *After:* "Our plan saves you $400."
4. **Cut adjectives — prove the claim instead.** *Before:* "Incredibly reliable engines." *After:* "No engine has failed in 30 years."
5. **Replace every vague word with a checkable fact.** *Before:* "Fast delivery." *After:* "Fresh, hot pizza in 30 minutes — or it's free."
6. **Shorter is stronger; cut until it hurts, then cut once more.** *Before:* "There has never been a better time to consider driving smaller." *After:* "Think small."
7. **Use periods, not commas — short sentences hit harder.** *Before:* "It's tough, durable, and built to last." *After:* "It's tough. It lasts."
8. **Put the punch word last — the end of the line is the loudest spot.** *Before:* "Lemon is what we call this car." *After:* "Lemon."
9. **Write the way you'd say it to one person, not announce it to a crowd.** *Before:* "Consumers deserve superior refreshment." *After:* "Thirsty?"
10. **Show, don't claim — dramatize the proof.** *Before:* "Very strong glue." *After:* "This ad is glued to the page. Try tearing it off."
11. **One idea per line; two ideas is zero ideas.** *Before:* "Cheap, fast, and eco-friendly flights." *After:* "London for £29."
12. **Name the reader or their problem so they know it's for them.** *Before:* "A better mattress." *After:* "To people who wake up tired."

## 3. Headline formulas that work

Old formulas survive because human curiosity doesn't change. From John Caples, Victor Schwab, and Ogilvy's testing.

1. **How-to.** Pattern: *How to [get desired result].* Example: "How to win friends and influence people."
2. **Question.** Pattern: *A question the reader must answer for themselves.* Example: "Do you make these mistakes in English?" (Note "these" — it forces you to read on.)
3. **News.** Pattern: *Announcing / Introducing / Now / Finally + the new thing.* Example: "Announcing the new Ford — quieter than a Rolls."
4. **Testimonial / first-person story.** Pattern: *A real voice saying something surprising.* Example: "They laughed when I sat down at the piano. But when I started to play —"
5. **Curiosity with payoff.** Pattern: *An odd statement the ad then explains.* Example: "'I never read The Economist.' Management trainee. Aged 42." The twist sells the product; the curiosity isn't empty.
6. **Specific claim + proof.** Pattern: *[Exact number or fact] that implies the benefit.* Example: "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock."
7. **Direct offer.** Pattern: *[Thing] for [price/terms], plain and flat.* Example: "London for £29 return." No cleverness needed when the deal is the story.
8. **Reason-why / numbered list.** Pattern: *[Number] ways/reasons to [benefit].* Example: "7 ways to cut your heating bill this winter." The number promises the copy is organized and finite.

## 4. Headline sins

1. **The pun without payoff.** Wordplay that's clever about words, not the product. *Fix:* keep the joke only if the punchline is the selling point; otherwise say the benefit straight. (Luke Sullivan: write the puns down, then flush them.)
2. **Brand-speak.** "Empowering solutions for tomorrow's world." No human talks like this, so no human reads it. *Fix:* say it out loud to one person; write down what you actually said.
3. **Claiming instead of showing.** "Highest quality." "Unmatched service." Claims are free, so readers discount them to zero. *Fix:* swap the claim for one piece of evidence — a number, a test, a demonstration.
4. **The tricky or blind headline.** A riddle that only makes sense after the body copy or the logo. Ogilvy: people read too fast to figure out what you mean. *Fix:* the headline must work alone, in one pass.
5. **Wordiness.** Every extra word is a reason to stop reading. *Fix:* delete words one at a time until the line breaks, then put the last one back.
6. **The interchangeable line.** It could run over any competitor's logo. *Fix:* start from something only this brand can truthfully say, and build the line from that.

---

## Taglines vs. headlines (know the difference)

- A **headline** sells one ad, one message, today. It changes with every ad.
- A **tagline** sums up the brand for years ("Just Do It"). It sits under the logo and changes almost never.
- Don't write a tagline when the job needs a headline: a vague timeless-sounding line on a single ad sells nothing. Headlines work harder; taglines just have longer contracts.
