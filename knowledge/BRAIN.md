# The Creative Director Brain

The distilled operating system of this tool, synthesized from eight research documents
(photography craft, advertising craft, digital/social craft, the print canon, the modern
canon, design-masters commentary, award-judge language, and practitioner teardowns).
This is what the analyzer and compiler actually know. Full sources live in this folder.

Rule zero, from the user: always reduce to shorter, simpler sentences without losing the core value.

---

## How the critic judges (in order)

1. **Where does the eye go first, second, third?** Sizes are a ranking. The viewer reads the ad in the order you sized it.
2. **What's the one idea?** Say it in one sentence. An ad that says three things says nothing.
3. **What do you feel?** Name the emotion. "Nothing" is the worst answer — feeling drives memory and sales.
4. **Cover the logo.** Still know whose ad it is? If any competitor could sign it, it builds nobody's brand.
5. **Would a stranger stop?** Judge for a distracted person on a phone with two seconds, not for a jury.
6. **What can be removed?** Keep cutting until removing one more thing breaks it.

Judge vocabulary in plain words: *insight* = a true, fresh "that's so me" observation. *Craft* = visible human care in every detail. *Tension* = two things that don't belong together. *Wallpaper* = pleasant, professional, invisible — the most common failure.

## The six dimensions

**Focal hierarchy** — one element must clearly dominate; the eye needs one obvious landing spot. Contrast is how the eye ranks things: big vs small, dark vs light. Two heroes means both lose. Faces and single products pull hardest at feed size.

**Layout** — alignment reads as trust; scatter reads as cheap. Grouping is meaning: near = related, far = unrelated. A grid gives every element a home; one deliberate grid-break creates energy, but only if the grid is obvious everywhere else. Z-path: headline top-left, sign-off bottom-right.

**Negative space** — emptiness is a spotlight, not waste. Small subject + vast emptiness = confidence and premium (the Think Small move). Cramped = discount bin. Empty space also makes text land harder.

**Color story** — restraint, then one accent. Roughly 60% base, 30% support, 10% accent; the accent owns the most important element. Monochrome palettes read as designed. A brand that repeats one color owns it (Economist red, Tiffany blue). Warm = cozy and human; cool desaturated = modern and premium; teal-orange = commercial energy.

**Typography** — type-led or image-led, never both loud. Two fonts, three sizes, max. Font personality is judged before a word is read: serif = classic trust, clean sans = modern, script = personal or luxe. Headline and image should complete each other, never repeat each other. On social: six words or fewer on screen, big, high-contrast.

**Concept** — the idea is the ad; everything else is delivery. The strongest engines: tension, juxtaposition, visual metaphor (say the benefit with an object swap), scale surprise, flaw flipped into a brag, and wit that lets the viewer finish it (give 2+2, not 4 — what people figure out themselves, they believe). Sell who the buyer becomes, not what the product does. Specificity beats cleverness: one checkable detail beats ten adjectives.

## Canon moves (name them when you see them)

- **Think Small** — tiny subject, vast emptiness. Whisper in a room of shouting.
- **Lemon** — open with an apparent self-insult, resolve it as a brag.
- **Economist** — the viewer completes the joke; getting it is the reward.
- **Rolls-Royce clock** — one hyper-specific, verifiable detail instead of adjectives.
- **Heinz fuse** — merge two things into one impossible object that states the message.
- **Absolut** — lock a strict visual formula, then riff on it forever.
- **iPod silhouette** — radical reduction: one shape, one flat color, one product detail.
- **Moldy Whopper** — break the category's most sacred image to prove a claim.
- **Dove split** — put two states side by side; the gap argues by itself.
- **Marlboro/Nike identity** — sell the person the buyer wants to be; the product can even be absent.
- **Got Milk absence** — dramatize the worst moment to be without it.
- **Show the receipt** — proof over promises: the number, the review, the screenshot.

## Craft signals: premium vs cheap

Premium: generous empty space; soft wrapping light or one deliberate hard-light look; one disciplined palette; shallow focus isolating the subject; real visible texture; slightly low camera angle; clean intentional reflections; everything aligned.
Cheap: stuffed frame; on-camera flash and blown highlights; clashing random colors; everything equally sharp; plastic over-retouching; careless phone angle; messy reflections; scattered elements.
Mediocre images silently reprice the product downward.

Photography levers, plain: soft light = calm and expensive. Hard light = bold and graphic. Rim light = sculpted edges on dark. Golden hour = warm nostalgia. Low angle = power. Macro = craftsmanship. Frozen splash = freshness and energy. Shallow depth of field = "this matters."

## The social lens (when the work is a feed ad)

- The first glance does 80% of the work. Would the frozen first frame stop a thumb?
- Legible when shrunk to thumbnail. Squint test: does the message survive?
- One message, one ask. Six or fewer words on screen.
- Keep text and logos out of the edges (app UI covers them).
- Native beats polished for cold audiences; polish wins for premium and warm audiences.
- Proof elements convert: stars, numbers, before/after, real customer words.
- Proven static formats: us-vs-them table, testimonial static, founder note, feature callout, before/after split, text-message screenshot, listicle, stat hero, lo-fi note, meme, offer card, grid collage.

## The words (headline craft)

Five times as many people read the headline as the body. Judge every line with six tests:
stranger (would a busy stranger stop?), specificity (checkable fact beats superlative),
picture (can you see it?), swap (could a competitor run it?), out-loud (human speech?),
so-what (does the reader gain anything?). Craft rules: benefit first, one idea per line,
active verbs, cut adjectives and prove instead, periods over commas, punch word last,
shorter is stronger. Sins: puns without payoff, brand-speak, claims without proof,
riddles that need the logo, wordiness, interchangeable lines.

## The grading engine (how the letter is earned)

The model never picks the letter. It classifies the upload (photo, print ad, social ad,
banner, packaging, organic post, UI), then scores that category's criteria 0-4 against
anchors where 2 = typical professional work. The server computes the grade from weights,
gates, and bands calibrated to real judging systems (see grading-photo.md, grading-ads.md):

- Center: all 2s = C. Typical work is a C, not a B — System1 testing shows ~half of
  viewers feel nothing when shown the average ad.
- Gates: no idea or no stop power → C+ ceiling. Dull (no emotional pull) → C+ ceiling.
  Brandless (cover the logo, could be anyone) → B- ceiling. Both emotion and branding
  must clear before top grades unlock.
- PPA rule: any single hard failure (a flaw a judge would name out loud) → below B+,
  no matter how strong the rest.
- A means portfolio/award-defensible — roughly the top few percent, per Cannes (~3-4%
  of entries win) and D&AD (~5.7% earn any Pencil).

## The writing rules (voice of every output)

- Short simple sentences. A smart 15-year-old gets it on first read.
- Every explanation says what the choice does *to the viewer*.
- No jargon without a five-word plain gloss.
- Fragments are generic, reusable techniques — never the ad's literal content, never brand names.
- Cut every word that isn't carrying weight.
