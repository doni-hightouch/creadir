# The demo arc — how Ad Studio gets explained to a cold audience

Source: Gong transcripts of Nate Argosh's Ad Studio demos. Grammarly/Superhuman
(Apr 27 pitch + May 11 full demo), Vuori creative team (May 13), Wild Alaskan
(May 20). Quotes verbatim.

## The repeatable playbook

**1. Discovery before demo, every time.** He maps current workflow, tools, and
bottlenecks — and critically, their comfort with AI editing — before showing
anything generative:

> "Maybe to start, it would be helpful to understand a little bit high level
> about y'all's processes... so I can help tailor the conversation."

Escalating questions: end-to-end creative workflow → biggest bottlenecks →
appetite for AI/self-serve → the creative team's stance → *"what is most
exciting and what is your biggest reservations or reasons why this might not
be a fit?"*

With Vuori (a creative team, brand-protective) he asked whether there was
"less appetite for AI editing things or generating things," then re-sequenced
the whole demo to lead with no-imagery-change use cases.

**2. Open with the market thesis, not the product.**

> "One of the common things that keeps coming up is how the performance
> equation has really changed over the past 18 months... success is really
> driven a lot by the creative. And these channels are so hungry for so much
> creative all the time. This constant stream of varied creative."
>
> "Like Meta says directly — as opposed to making these small subtle tweaks to
> the same ad, they ask for these really different ads in look and feel and
> storyline."

The core positioning sentence:

> "One of the things that we're really trying to solve here with Ad Studio is
> to help you create content variety that still is on brand and governed —
> things that your brand would be happy and proud to launch."

**3. The context layer is the star, not generation.** The through-line in all
four calls:

> "One of the biggest learnings we've had, and as I'm sure some of y'all have
> seen with other AI tools, is you only get out sort of the quality of what
> you put in. And so this system is really sitting on top of all of this rich
> context about your business."
>
> "One of the big differentiators of Hightouch is that it's sitting on top of
> all of this context — your marketing context, the channel data, the customer
> data, your creative assets, your brand context. And so you can use all of
> that to really inform this virtuous loop of insights and creative."

An SE put it bluntly: the context layer is *"really just our wedge against a
lot of the gen AI tools out there."*

**4. Show the end state first, then back up.** Wild Alaskan: *"I just wanna
start with the end state... this is the example of the kind of output that you
might be able to generate."* Also used at DraftKings ("started with the end in
mind"). Then collect a live prompt from the customer early, let it run in the
background for the whole call, and harvest it late — batches of ~10 take 5–15
minutes.

**5. The canonical arc: insight → prompt → asset → edit → resize → launch.**
The single most-used bridge move is pasting a report link into the ad chat:
*"I just pasted the link of the report and said let's make 10 ads for that
angle that we found in this trend."*

## How he teaches prompting to non-AI people

His anti-prompt-engineering message, the most important line for a novice
audience — given in direct answer to *"how much hand holding do we have to do
for this?"*:

> "I think the biggest differentiator or the biggest eye opener for marketing
> teams that get on here is like how much it just kind of works out of the
> box. Like with a lot of AI tools, you have to be very specific with your
> prompts — the lighting angle and all these different things. A lot of that
> is just baked in. So it can take a high level prompt and just figure it out."

And the passive/directed distinction:

> "By default, it has the ability to just pull in lots of different
> insights... you give it a high level ask like 'I want to create 10 new
> assets for Grammarly,' and it can take that high level ask and look at the
> past ads that you've ran, what's performing well, look at competitors in the
> space and use that to flesh out your prompt into concrete ideas... But then
> you can also just be more direct — say 'I want to riff on this competitor.'"

**Every real demo prompt he types is one or two plain sentences.** This is the
best possible teaching material because it destroys the "prompting is
technical" assumption:

- "Take this ad and recreate it using five different template layouts
  available. Don't change the imagery at all."
- "Go look at my website and come up with a bunch of diverse concepts covering
  different value props."
- "Let's make 10 ads for that angle that we found in this trend."
- "Let's try some creative layouts that break outside the bounds of our
  existing templates" (+ nudge words: *editorial*, *fashion magazine*).
- "I gave it a reference image and asked it to come up with different ads that
  feel like they're part of this shoot but showcase different products and
  different scenes."
- "Swap this out with another salmon picture from my asset library."
- "Go look at the latest ButcherBox report and pull the ad with the split
  screen and let's create some riffs on that."
- "Hey, for insight number three about the challenger hooks — do we have any
  other challenger hooks in our assets in brand folder? Let's pull those in."

## The 80/20 pitch that defuses the #1 AI fear

Verbatim in three of four calls, and the thing customers consistently name as
the differentiator:

> "Oftentimes getting 80 percent of the way there is easy with a lot of AI
> tools, but the last 20 percent is super annoying. You're prompting back and
> forth a 1,000 times and it's messing things up. So if you just want to swap
> out a word or change punctuation, you can just do that within the UI."
>
> "This is often one of the biggest challenges we see customers having with
> other AI creation platforms — it creates these flat images and then you're
> kind of left to endlessly prompt your way to the last few little tweaks."

Customer confirmation: *"this one actually looks directionally better. I do
like that you can go in and update the layers... in the Meta tool that's
similar, it spits out a bunch of options and then it's like, oh well, it'd look
good if I could just take this piece out."* And the pain in their own words:
*"we also have been making a lot of 80 percent ready assets... the last 20
percent is taking us forever."*

## Guardrails explained as a dial, not a switch

> "We've built the system in a really flexible way where you can really fine
> tune how much leeway you give to the AI. Does it just use your existing
> assets exactly as is? And maybe it's only allowed to move around text and
> overlays... And if you want to give the system ability to swap out the color
> of the short on this model, you can start to give a little more leeway."

There's a literal editable/not-editable toggle per asset. This is how he
handles brand-protective creative teams.

## Designer-replacement fear: handled with a role reframe, never denial

Asked whether creative teams should be included:

> "We definitely see the most success when the creative team is bought in and
> brought along for the ride... we want it to be a tool that helps remove some
> of those more manual tasks from the creative team's plate so that they can
> focus on bigger efforts... usually we see the most success when they help set
> up the guardrails of the system, and they're actually there to approve the
> outputs."

Alternative framing offered directly to creatives:

> "We have some creative teams that are using the system as sort of a creative
> thought partner — when you're brainstorming new concepts, it can just help
> you explore lots of different directions at once."

Customers voice the fear openly: *"It seems like we might put our creative team
out of the job with this one."* / *"I could see a creative team being a little
bit weary about giving the marketing team access to make their own creative and
launch it without any oversight."* / *"we're always going to work with our
internal team... there's a human component there that we're not going to
necessarily replace."*

## "How did it know?" — the provenance moment

The single best-documented aha. A Vuori designer couldn't believe the copy:
*"But how did it know new arrivals? How did it know made with bliss blend...
and how did it know French roast?"* The answer:

> "This is kind of the magic of this slide — all of the context that we've
> given the system... I've given it a whole document with a bunch of context
> about Vuori, and it can also access your website... it also has some subset
> of your product catalog... It also works the other way around. If you upload
> an image, it can go look at your product catalog and be like, this is the
> All The Feels wrap top, and pull in that copy and that name so that it knows
> what it is."

Then he opens the agent run log live:

> "Behind the scenes, it's not just calling one AI tool — there's this whole
> system... it's going and searching through all of the product catalog. It's
> then kicking off these different ad generation tasks and you can see it's
> come up with different prompts. It's pulled in the exact files of the imagery
> that it's supposed to use and the different templates."

Result: skepticism → *"So it pulled that in on its own... Interesting.
Thank you."*

## Radical transparency as a trust device

- Opens the run log to show sub-agents, prompts, pulled files.
- Discloses his own edits: *"in the spirit of transparency, I made one edit
  here. It had a Vuori logo in a weird spot. So I just asked it to remove it."*
- Caveats every mocked-up brand: *"it's not connected to your data. It's my
  best attempt at your brand."*
- Names roadmap gaps openly (approvals workflow, Figma paste bug, DPA
  previews finicky).
- States traction honestly: *"This is one of our newer products, but we have
  like 12 customers at this point."*
- **Invites criticism**: *"Any reactions to the quality of these assets?...
  do these feel close to being brand approved or a ways off?"* and *"if it's
  not exciting, that's a totally valid answer as well."*

## Concept explanations worth reusing verbatim

**Projects:** *"A project is just a set of assets that you're working on —
similar to like a Figma file."*

**Insights:** *"It looks across all your creative and tries to pull out these
higher level insights and takeaways that are not just like this ad performed
better than that ad."* Two grounding stories: a printed "full kit" beating
individual items; fintech UGC with an app-UI overlay beating plain UGC — and
the punchline, *"that was not in the naming of the ads. It had to go look at
all the different content and figure out where there's interesting
divergences."*

**Signals (external trends):** *"It can go and look at Reddit and Twitter and
just do open web searches to figure out what are some interesting signals out
there in the world relevant to your brand that might pose an interesting
opportunity for a new angle."* Sources are clickable.

**Semantic asset search:** *"You can say just swap this out with another salmon
picture from my asset library... It does sort of a semantic search. So it can
actually look at these images, figure out what's in them even if you don't
have it tagged as salmon."*

**Safe zones on resize:** *"The system already has a native understanding of
safe zones... for the 9:16, it moved the logo up a bit from the bottom, but you
can always add more guidance."*

**Video is assembled, not just generated:** *"When it outputs a final video,
it's not just one video that's returned from an AI model. It's our agent
assembling that video... it could also just be slicing up other existing
things."* And: *"Some of the strongest outputs that we've seen are when people
have existing assets that they're asking the system to remix or make edits to."*

**Context files:** *"We have this super flexible context layer where you can
upload just raw context into the system... whenever the agent is analyzing
data, what are some common rules and norms that it should follow? Here is our
standard naming convention. Here are the top metrics that we care about across
each stage of our funnel."*

**Everything is reachable:** *"Everything that you see in Hightouch, basically,
the agent can access."*

## The AI-literacy questions customers actually ask

Direct FAQ seeds, all verbatim:

- "How much hand holding do we have to do for this?"
- "How did it know [product name / value prop]?"
- "Does it have shared or saved memory? If we say never include this in an ad,
  will it remember that going forward?"
- "Does it understand best practices for each platform, or do we need to
  specify this is for Meta?"
- "Can you be more click-and-go instead of fully prompting? That'll be a lot
  easier."
- "Is that something we can feed it... or is it kind of just we get what
  we get?" (about safe zones)
- "Can you share what model this platform is using?"
- "How much work went in the front end of uploading everything you needed to
  output these?"
- "How does it map the creatives to that data? Do we need to set up
  identifiers?"
- Consistency across sizes: *"the shadows are different in the background — how
  can you create consistency within all the assets that you're extending in an
  ad set?"*
- Product mix-ups: *"a black core short might look pretty similar to a black
  bank short... how well does the tool do making sure it's not mixing and
  matching product feature callouts onto the image of the wrong short?"*
- Legal/model contracts: *"this photo isn't part of that model's contract
  because it was generated with AI. So I'm assuming there's some gray area?"*
- "Garbage in garbage out" — stated by customers themselves as the mental
  model they already have.

## What reliably produces "wow"

Automatic size variants respecting safe zones; layer-level editability; semantic
DAM search; insight reports that cite their sources; competitor analysis that
goes beyond the raw ad library; Slack-delivered scheduled reports; video
slicing and recombination; and launching to channels from inside the tool.

## The customer's own articulation of the core value

Worth leading a guide with, because it's a buyer's words not a vendor's:

> "The initial bare bones of what I had envisioned for this is just the ability
> to quickly create and iterate upon net new and existing static ads... From
> the inception of the idea to the posting of the static in Meta to the first
> dollar that's spent, that process can take anywhere from five to 10 business
> days... everything else is really nice to have."
