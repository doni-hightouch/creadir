# How to Judge an Ad Like the Best Judges Do

A crash course in the language and standards of Cannes Lions and D&AD jurors, the great creative directors (Trott, Arden, Lois, Sullivan), and the effectiveness scientists (Sharp, Field, Wood). No jargon without a plain-English translation.

---

## 1. The Language of Great Judges

These are the words judges actually use in the room. Learn them and you can critique any ad.

1. **The idea** — the one thought the ad exists to plant. If you can't say it in a sentence, there isn't one.
2. **Insight** — a true, fresh observation about how people actually feel or behave. It makes viewers think "that's so me."
3. **Truth** — the ad rings honest about the product or about life. Fake sentiment is spotted instantly and punished.
4. **Craft** — the quality of the making: writing, photography, typography, sound, edit. Judges say great craft is where you *feel* the human care.
5. **Simplicity** — one message, ruthlessly stripped. Dave Trott's rule: an ad that says three things says nothing.
6. **Single-minded** — same idea, as a discipline: everything in the ad serves the one thought; everything else is cut.
7. **Impact / stopping power** — does it get noticed at all? Trott: most advertising fails not by offending but by being invisible.
8. **Tension** — two things that don't belong together (a surprise, a conflict). Luke Sullivan: no tension, no interest.
9. **Distinctive** — it could only be this brand. Cover the logo: if you still know who it's for, it's distinctive.
10. **Brave** — the client bought something risky instead of safe. Juries reward nerve; Paul Arden: being right is not enough, be remarkable.
11. **Fresh** — you haven't seen it before. Juries kill anything that smells like last year's winner.
12. **Emotional** — it makes you *feel* something, not just understand something. Feeling drives memory and sales.
13. **Fit for purpose** — D&AD's third question: does it actually do the job the brief asked for?
14. **Wallpaper** — the insult. Pleasant, professional, invisible. The most common failure, and the worst.
15. **Scam ad** — work made only to win awards, barely run for a real client. The dirty word of judging rooms.

D&AD juries boil it all down to three questions: **Is it a brilliant idea? Is it beautifully crafted? Is it fit for purpose?**

## 2. What Wins Awards vs. What Sells

Be honest about this tension.

- **Juries reward novelty.** They see thousands of entries, so surprise, cleverness, and cultural buzz win. Sales results are often an afterthought.
- **The market rewards memory.** Sales come from being noticed, felt, remembered, and clearly branded — sometimes by ads juries find boring.
- **The gap is real.** Peter Field's IPA research found award-winning campaigns were once ~12x more efficient than average; by 2019 the advantage had collapsed. He called it "the crisis in creative effectiveness" — creativity misused for short-term buzz instead of long-term brand building.
- **But the best work does both.** The all-time greats (VW "Lemon", "Dumb Ways to Die", Snickers "You're Not You") won big *and* sold big. Creativity isn't the enemy of effectiveness — it's the multiplier, when pointed at the brand.

**Judge's takeaway:** ask *both* "would a jury applaud this?" and "would a distracted shopper remember whose ad it was?"

## 3. The Effectiveness Science in Plain Words

### Distinctive brand assets (Byron Sharp / Ehrenberg-Bass)
A brand's ownable triggers: its colors, logo, character, sound, pack shape. They let people recognize the brand fast, without reading. Assets only work if used consistently for years.
**For a single image ad:** the brand's look should be unmistakable at a glance — cover the logo and test it.

### Emotion beats persuasion (System1)
"When people feel more, they buy more." Emotional response predicts long-term sales better than message recall or logical argument. Ads that leave you neutral leave the market unchanged.
**For a single image ad:** it must trigger a fast feeling — a smile, a pang, a gasp — not just deliver a claim.

### Right-brain features (Orlando Wood, "Lemon")
Ads full of "right-brain" features — living characters, faces, a real sense of place, music, humor, unfolding human moments — attract broad, lasting attention and grow brands. Flat "left-brain" ads (product close-ups, word lists, voiceover claims, abstract graphics) get filtered out.
**For a single image ad:** show a person, a face, a place, a moment — not just the product on a white void with copy.

### Mental availability (Ehrenberg-Bass)
Brands grow by being easily thought of in buying moments, by as many category buyers as possible. Ads build memory links between the brand and the situations where people buy. Reach and memorability beat preaching to fans.
**For a single image ad:** link the brand to a clear buying situation ("long drive", "late night hunger") so it comes to mind when it matters.

## 4. The 8-Question Judging Card

Score any ad, 1–5 on each. Total it. Trust the low scores — one fatal flaw sinks an ad.

1. **Would I stop?** Does it grab attention in a world that ignores ads?
2. **What's the one idea?** Can I say it in one sentence? If not, fail.
3. **Is there a real insight?** Does it reveal something true about people, or just describe the product?
4. **Do I feel anything?** Name the emotion. "Nothing" is the worst answer.
5. **Cover the logo — do I still know the brand?** If any competitor could sign it, it's building nobody's brand.
6. **Have I seen this before?** Fresh idea, or a borrowed trick?
7. **Is the craft loved?** Does every detail — word, image, type — show human care?
8. **Would it work on a stranger?** Not on the jury, not on the client — on a distracted person who doesn't care, in one second.

**The shortcut version, from the masters:** Trott — did anyone *notice* it? Arden — is it *remarkable* or merely right? Sharp — will they *remember whose it was*? If it fails any of those three, nothing else matters.
