# Creative Direction for Digital & Social Ads
### A field guide for beginners: Meta, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube thumbnails, display, and DOOH

**The one idea behind everything here:** people scroll fast, on a phone, with the sound off. Your ad has about 1–3 seconds to earn a pause. Every rule below exists to win that moment.

---

## Part 1: The 25 concepts that matter

### A. Stopping the thumb (attention)

**1. The 3-second hook**
The first moment decides everything — viewers scroll past in under 2 seconds, and TikTok says the first seconds carry ~90% of ad recall. Lead with the most interesting frame, not a logo build-up.
> Prompt phrase: *"open on the most surprising moment, no intro"*

**2. First-frame contrast (pattern interrupt)**
A pattern interrupt (something visually unlike the posts around it) breaks the feed's sameness, so the eye snags on it. Use a bold color, odd angle, or unexpected object in frame one.
> Prompt phrase: *"bold high-contrast first frame, unexpected visual, no slow fade"*

**3. Faces stop scrolls**
Human brains are wired to lock onto faces; on YouTube, thumbnails with expressive faces lift click-through rate 20–30%. Use a real face with a big, readable emotion.
> Prompt phrase: *"expressive human face, close-up, emotion readable at thumbnail size"*

**4. Direct eye contact and direct address**
Looking into the lens and saying "you" feels like a person talking to the viewer, not a brand broadcasting. It's the cheapest hook there is.
> Prompt phrase: *"person looking into camera, speaking directly to viewer"*

**5. Motion implication (for still images)**
Even a static image can suggest movement — a mid-pour, mid-jump, or mid-splash frame reads as "something is happening," which pulls attention. Frozen action beats posed product.
> Prompt phrase: *"frozen mid-action moment, product in motion, dynamic angle"*

**6. The curiosity gap**
Show or say something incomplete (a question the viewer needs answered) and the brain wants closure. "I didn't believe this until..." works; just make sure the ad pays it off, or people feel tricked.
> Prompt phrase: *"open a question in frame one, answer it later"*

**7. The big product close-up (hero shot)**
At feed size, small things vanish. One product, filling most of the frame, is instantly understood while scrolling.
> Prompt phrase: *"big product close-up, headline readable at thumbnail size"*

**8. Movement in the first two seconds (video)**
Static openings read as images and get skipped; quick cuts, a zoom, or a hand entering frame signal "this is a video worth watching."
> Prompt phrase: *"visible motion in first 2 seconds, fast first cut"*

### B. Saying one thing clearly (message and hierarchy)

**9. One ad, one message**
Mobile viewers give you a 3-second read; two messages means zero messages land. Pick a single benefit and cut everything else.
> Prompt phrase: *"single benefit, one focal point, nothing competing"*

**10. Design for sound-off**
Most feed video plays muted by default, so the ad must work as a silent film. Use captions, on-screen text, and visuals that tell the story alone — then add sound as a bonus layer.
> Prompt phrase: *"story works muted, burned-in captions, sound optional"*

**11. Text overlay: few, big, high-contrast**
Overlay text (words placed on top of the image or video) competes with tiny screens; keep it to roughly 3–6 words at a time, large, in a high-contrast color, ideally on a solid or semi-transparent backing bar.
> Prompt phrase: *"max 5 words on screen, bold, high-contrast backing"*

**12. One focal point (visual hierarchy)**
Visual hierarchy means guiding the eye in order: hook → product → text → button. If everything shouts, nothing is heard; make one element clearly biggest.
> Prompt phrase: *"one dominant element, clear size order, generous empty space"*

**13. Front-load the brand and product**
Skippers still see your first seconds, so brand or product shown early means even a "wasted" view builds memory. Meta's guidance: brand within the first 3 seconds.
> Prompt phrase: *"brand or product visible within first 3 seconds"*

**14. One clear CTA**
A CTA (call to action — the thing you want them to do) works when it's a single, plain verb phrase: "Shop now," "Get the app." One button, one action, high contrast.
> Prompt phrase: *"single action-verb CTA, contrasting button, end card"*

**15. Show, don't claim (demonstration)**
Seeing the product work — a before/after, a stain lifting, an app doing the task — is proof; a slogan is just a claim. Demo hooks are among TikTok's top performers.
> Prompt phrase: *"real-time demo, visible before and after"*

**16. Social proof**
Reviews, star ratings, "10,000 sold," or a real customer's words borrow trust the brand hasn't earned yet. Strangers believe strangers.
> Prompt phrase: *"real review quote on screen, star rating visible"*

### C. Fitting the format (craft rules)

**17. Vertical-first, fill the screen**
A 9:16 (full phone screen, tall) video occupies all the pixels a phone has; TikTok reports vertical-native content earns far more attention than repurposed horizontal. Shoot tall, don't letterbox.
> Prompt phrase: *"shot natively vertical 9:16, subject fills frame"*

**18. Respect safe zones**
Safe zones are the areas of the screen the app's own buttons don't cover. On 9:16, the top ~10–14% and bottom ~20–35%, plus the right edge on TikTok, get covered by UI — keep text and logos in the middle band.
> Prompt phrase: *"all text inside center safe zone, edges clear"*

**19. The shrink test (legibility at feed scale)**
Feed scale means the tiny size your ad actually renders at — a YouTube thumbnail is ~320×180 pixels on a phone. Shrink your creative to phone size before approving it; if you squint, it fails.
> Prompt phrase: *"legible when shrunk to phone-feed size"*

**20. Match the placement, don't stretch one asset**
Each placement (feed, Stories, Reels, thumbnail) has its own shape and habits; cropping one master file into all of them cuts heads off and hides text. Build 1:1, 4:5, and 9:16 versions on purpose.
> Prompt phrase: *"deliver dedicated 1:1, 4:5, and 9:16 crops"*

### D. Choosing a style (native vs polished)

**21. Native-feeling creative**
Native means "looks like a normal post from a person, not an ad." It wins for cold audiences and prospecting because it doesn't trigger ad-blindness — TikTok's own mantra is "don't make ads, make TikToks."
> Prompt phrase: *"casual phone-shot look, feels like a friend's post"*

**22. UGC-style creative**
UGC (user-generated content: real-person, selfie-style video) is native creative's workhorse — a person talking to camera about a product. It's cheap ($150–600 vs thousands for studio), fast, and agencies commonly run 60–70% UGC for prospecting.
> Prompt phrase: *"selfie-style testimonial, natural light, unscripted tone"*

**23. Polished creative — and when it wins**
Studio polish (professional lighting, design, and finish) wins when trust and desire matter most: retargeting warm audiences, premium products, hero brand moments, and anywhere quality itself is the message. Rough style selling a luxury item backfires.
> Prompt phrase: *"clean studio hero shot, premium lighting, brand palette"*

**24. A hook library, not one hook**
The same product can open a dozen ways: problem call-out ("Still doing X the hard way?"), bold claim, question, before/after, POV, myth-bust, "3 reasons why." Different hooks catch different people; rotate them.
> Prompt phrase: *"same body, five different 2-second openings"*

### E. Spending like the big spenders (testing heuristics)

**25. Test concepts before variations**
A concept is a genuinely different idea (new angle, new hook, new format); a variation is a small tweak (color, headline). Big spenders test broad concepts first, then make variations of only the winners — heavy Meta advertisers ship 20–40+ creatives a month.
> Prompt phrase: *"three distinct concepts, then iterate the winner"*

**26. Read hook rate and hold rate, then refresh**
Hook rate (% of viewers who watch past 3 seconds) tells you if the opening works; hold rate (% still watching later) tells you if the story works. Creative fatigue (performance dropping because everyone has seen the ad) is normal — fix the failing part and replace ads on a steady cadence instead of guessing.
> Prompt phrase: *"weak hook rate: swap only the first 3 seconds"*

## Part 2: The Feed Test — would this stop a thumb?

Run every ad through this before it ships. Any "no" means rework.

- [ ] **Shrink it.** At phone-feed size, can you tell what's happening in 1 second?
- [ ] **Mute it.** Does the message land with zero sound?
- [ ] **Freeze frame one.** Would that single frame stop a scroll on its own?
- [ ] **Count the seconds.** Is the hook (face, product, claim, or surprise) inside second 0–3?
- [ ] **Count the messages.** Is there exactly one?
- [ ] **Count the words on screen.** Six or fewer at any moment?
- [ ] **Check the edges.** Is all text and branding inside the safe zone?
- [ ] **Find the CTA.** Is there one obvious next step?
- [ ] **The neighbor test.** Placed between two organic posts, does it earn a look — or scream "ad"?
- [ ] **The payoff test.** If the hook makes a promise, does the ad keep it?

## Part 3: Per-format quick rules

### 1:1 — Square (1080×1080) · Feed
- Works everywhere; the safe default for feed images and carousels.
- Center the subject; keep a margin around all text.
- Best for: product hero shots, carousels, display-style promos.

### 4:5 — Vertical feed (1080×1350) · Facebook/Instagram Feed
- The tallest format feed allows — it takes more screen than 1:1, so prefer it for feed video and images.
- Same composition rules as 1:1, just more vertical room for the product.
- Don't reuse a 9:16 file here; the crop will cut off text.

### 9:16 — Full vertical (1080×1920) · Stories, Reels, TikTok
- Fills the whole phone screen; shoot natively vertical.
- Safe zone: keep text and logos in the middle band — avoid roughly the top 10–14%, the bottom 20–35% (captions, CTA, buttons live there), and TikTok's right edge (like/share icons).
- Hook text top-center in seconds 0–3; CTA in the last 2–3 seconds, above the UI.
- Captions on by default; assume muted playback.

### YouTube thumbnails (1280×720, seen at ~320×180)
- One face with a big emotion, or one big object — never both small.
- Max 3–5 words of text, huge, high contrast (white with dark outline is the reliable default).
- Thumbnail and title should say *different* things that work together.
- Approve only after viewing at actual mobile size.

### Display banners
- One benefit, one image, one CTA button — readable in 2–3 seconds.
- Contrast the CTA button color against everything else.
- Fonts must survive the smallest slot (~320px wide).

### DOOH (digital out-of-home: big public screens and billboards)
- Seven words or fewer, total. Drivers get ~3–10 seconds.
- Extreme contrast: light text on dark, or white on one saturated color.
- Logo big, message bigger. No fine print, no QR codes on highway boards.
- Test: stand at real viewing distance, look for 3 seconds, look away — what do you remember?

## Part 4: Do / Don't

**Do**
- Open on the payoff, hook first, explain later.
- Design for mute; caption everything.
- Show the product doing its job.
- Make one element unmistakably dominant.
- Build each aspect ratio on purpose.
- Test wildly different concepts, then iterate winners.
- Use real people and real language for cold audiences.
- Refresh creative before fatigue kills performance.

**Don't**
- Don't open with a logo animation or slow establishing shot.
- Don't put text near screen edges (UI will cover it).
- Don't cram two messages or two CTAs into one ad.
- Don't use text smaller than you can read at arm's length on a phone.
- Don't stretch one 16:9 video across every placement.
- Don't over-polish TikTok prospecting creative — it reads as an ad and gets skipped.
- Don't run UGC-rough style for premium retargeting — match polish to trust stage.
- Don't judge creative on opinions; judge it on hook rate, hold rate, and cost per result.
- Don't bait with a hook the ad never pays off.
